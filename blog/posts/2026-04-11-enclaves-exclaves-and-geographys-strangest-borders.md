An enclave is a territory entirely surrounded by another country. An exclave is a part of a country that is physically separated from the main body by foreign territory. Some places are both. And a few places in the world involve enclaves within enclaves within enclaves, creating geographic nesting dolls that challenge the very concept of a coherent border.

These anomalies are not mistakes. They are the products of history, treaties, and the stubborn refusal of political boundaries to follow geographic logic.

## The India-Bangladesh Border

Before a landmark land swap in 2015, the India-Bangladesh border contained 162 enclaves, including the world's only third-order enclave: a piece of India inside a piece of Bangladesh inside a piece of India inside Bangladesh. This recursive territory, called Dahala Khagrabari, covered less than one hectare of land.

The enclaves were relics of a series of treaties and territorial bets between local rulers dating back to the Mughal Empire. When India and Pakistan (later Bangladesh) partitioned in 1947, these feudal arrangements were frozen into international borders. Residents of the enclaves were effectively stateless, unable to access schools, hospitals, or government services in the surrounding country and cut off from their nominal homeland.

In 2015, India and Bangladesh exchanged 162 enclaves, allowing roughly 50,000 residents to choose their nationality. It was one of the most complex border adjustments in modern history.

## Kaliningrad: Russia's European Exclave

Kaliningrad Oblast is a Russian territory on the Baltic Sea, wedged between Lithuania and Poland. It has no land connection to Russia. To travel from Kaliningrad to the nearest Russian territory by land, you must cross at least one foreign country.

Kaliningrad was formerly Konigsberg, the historic capital of East Prussia. It was annexed by the Soviet Union in 1945 after World War II, and the German population was expelled and replaced by Soviet settlers. When Lithuania declared independence in 1990, Kaliningrad was suddenly separated from Russia by a foreign country.

The territory presents unique logistical and political challenges. Russian citizens traveling by train between Kaliningrad and Moscow must transit through Lithuania, requiring special documentation. The territory is also one of NATO's most sensitive pressure points, surrounded by alliance members on all sides.

## Baarle-Hertog and Baarle-Nassau

The Belgian town of Baarle-Hertog and the Dutch town of Baarle-Nassau occupy the same geographic area in an arrangement so complex that some buildings are divided between two countries. The border runs through houses, shops, and restaurants. By convention, a building's nationality is determined by the location of its front door.

The arrangement dates to medieval treaties between the Duke of Brabant and the Lord of Breda, which divided land ownership in ways that never aligned with a clean border. When Belgium and the Netherlands became separate countries in 1830, these feudal land parcels became international enclaves. There are 22 Belgian enclaves within the Netherlands and 8 Dutch counter-enclaves within those Belgian enclaves.

Residents have adapted pragmatically. The towns share infrastructure, and citizens of both countries go about daily life with minimal friction. Street markings indicate where the border crosses roads. It is one of the friendliest and most absurd international borders in the world.

## Ceuta and Melilla

Spain's two autonomous cities on the North African coast are not technically enclaves, since they have coastlines, but they function similarly: European territories surrounded on their land sides by Morocco. The cities have been Spanish longer than some parts of Spain itself, with Ceuta under Iberian control since 1668 and Melilla since 1497.

Morocco claims both cities, and the borders are marked by tall fences, razor wire, and extensive surveillance. The boundaries are among the most fortified in the world and serve as the only land borders between Africa and the European Union.

## Nahwa: The Counter-Enclave

The UAE village of Nahwa is an Emirati enclave inside the Omani exclave of Madha, which is itself surrounded by UAE territory. This makes Nahwa a counter-enclave, a territory of one country inside a territory of another country that is inside the first country again.

The arrangement exists because traditional tribal territories in the region did not follow neat geographic boundaries. When the UAE and Oman formalized their borders, these tribal allegiances were preserved as sovereign enclaves.

## Llívia: Spain's Island in France

The town of Llívia, population roughly 1,500, is a Spanish enclave entirely surrounded by French territory in the eastern Pyrenees. It exists because of the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659, which ceded 33 villages from Spain to France. Llívia was classified as a town, not a village, and was therefore excluded from the transfer.

A 1.6-kilometer neutral road connects Llívia to Spain, running through French territory. The road is technically international and cannot be blocked by either country. Residents of Llívia live in Spain, drive through France to reach the rest of their country, and shop in whichever nation offers better prices.

## Why Enclaves Exist

Enclaves and exclaves persist because borders are historical documents, not geographic ones. They record the outcomes of treaties, wars, marriages, and feudal arrangements that made sense in their original context but create anomalies in the modern system of sovereign nation-states.

Eliminating them requires bilateral negotiation, population consent, and often the kind of political will that is difficult to muster for small, obscure territories. Most countries choose to live with the inconvenience rather than reopen settled borders.

For geography enthusiasts, enclaves are a reminder that the map is a human invention. The lines on it are not natural features. They are decisions, and some of those decisions were very strange indeed.
