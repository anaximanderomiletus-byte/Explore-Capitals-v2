How long is the coastline of Norway? The CIA World Factbook says 25,148 kilometers. Other sources say 58,133 kilometers. Still others put it above 100,000 kilometers. These are not errors. They are different answers to the same question, each using a different method of measurement.

The coastline paradox, first described mathematically by Lewis Fry Richardson in the 1950s and later formalized by Benoit Mandelbrot, states that the measured length of a coastline depends on the scale of measurement. The smaller the ruler, the longer the coastline. This is not a technical quibble. It reveals something fundamental about the nature of geographic features.

## The Paradox Explained

Imagine measuring a coastline with a ruler 100 kilometers long. You would span straight lines from point to point, cutting across bays and headlands. The result would be a relatively short measurement. Now repeat the exercise with a one-kilometer ruler. Each smaller strait and inlet would add length. With a one-meter ruler, every rock and crevice would contribute. With a one-centimeter ruler, every grain of sand would matter.

The length increases with every reduction in scale, and there is no natural stopping point. Unlike a circle or a straight line, a coastline has no definitive length. It has a length at a given resolution.

This is why Norway's coastline can be both 25,000 and 100,000 kilometers. Norway's coast is deeply fractured by fjords, islands, and peninsulas. At a coarse resolution, the fjords are averaged out. At a fine resolution, every twist and inlet adds distance. Countries with smooth, regular coastlines (like Libya) show much less variation between measurements.

## Fractal Geometry

Mandelbrot recognized that coastlines behave like fractals: shapes that exhibit self-similar complexity at every scale. Zoom into a coastline on a satellite image, and the jagged pattern looks similar whether you are viewing a 1,000-kilometer stretch or a 10-meter stretch. This self-similarity is the mathematical signature of a fractal.

Mandelbrot assigned fractal dimensions to coastlines. A perfectly smooth coastline would have a dimension of 1 (a simple line). A maximally jagged coastline would approach 2 (filling a plane). Most real coastlines fall between 1.0 and 1.4. Norway, with its intricate fjord system, has a higher fractal dimension than Egypt, whose Mediterranean coast is relatively straight.

## Rankings Depend on the Ruler

The consequence is that coastline rankings change depending on the measurement standard. Canada usually tops the list with roughly 202,000 kilometers of coastline at the resolution used by international databases. Indonesia, Norway, Russia, and the Philippines follow. But these rankings would shift significantly if a finer or coarser resolution were used.

Some measurements include islands. Others do not. Some trace the mainland coast only. Others follow every island, islet, and rock. The decision about what counts as "coast" is as important as the scale of measurement.

Australia's coastline is roughly 25,760 kilometers if you measure the mainland. Including islands, it exceeds 34,000 kilometers. The difference, nearly 10,000 kilometers, is entirely a question of what you choose to measure.

## Practical Implications

The coastline paradox is not just theoretical. It affects policy. Maritime boundaries, fishing rights, and territorial waters are all calculated from coastal baselines. A longer measured coastline can mean a larger exclusive economic zone, which means more control over offshore resources.

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea uses straight baselines drawn between headlands to simplify the problem. These baselines are political choices, not geographic measurements, and they can significantly expand a country's maritime claims. Norway, with its deeply indented coast, draws baselines across fjord mouths, enclosing vast areas of water as internal territory.

## Why It Matters for Geography

The coastline paradox teaches a broader lesson: maps are not reality. They are simplified representations of reality at a chosen scale and resolution. Every map involves decisions about what to include and what to omit, what to measure and how to measure it.

A globe at your desk shows smooth coastlines. A nautical chart shows every reef and sandbar. A satellite image shows the fractal complexity of the actual shore. All three are accurate. None is complete. The map you use determines the geography you see.

For anyone studying geography, the coastline paradox is a useful reminder that precision and accuracy are not the same thing. A number can be precisely calculated and still be meaningless without context. The question is not "how long is the coastline?" but "how long is the coastline at this resolution, for this purpose?"
