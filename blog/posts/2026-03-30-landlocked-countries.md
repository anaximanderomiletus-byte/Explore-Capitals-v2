Open a world map and trace the coastlines. Every continent is ringed by nations with ports, beaches, and direct access to the world's oceans. But look inland and you will find 44 countries that are entirely surrounded by other countries, with no coastline at all. These landlocked nations face a unique set of geographic challenges that affect everything from trade and military strategy to tourism and national identity.

Being landlocked is one of the most consequential geographic facts a country can have. Here is why.

## The Trade Problem

Over 80 percent of global trade by volume moves by sea. Container ships are the backbone of the world economy, carrying everything from electronics to grain across oceans for a fraction of the cost of air or land transport. Countries with ports can load and unload goods directly. Landlocked countries cannot.

Every import and export from a landlocked country must cross at least one international border by land before reaching a port. This adds cost, time, and political risk. If a neighboring country closes its border due to a dispute, raises transit fees, or simply has poor road infrastructure, the landlocked nation's entire economy can be squeezed.

The United Nations recognizes this disadvantage formally. The UN Office of the High Representative for the Least Developed Countries maintains a special program for Landlocked Developing Countries, acknowledging that geography itself is a barrier to economic growth.

## Africa's Landlocked Belt

Africa has more landlocked countries than any other continent: 16 in total. They stretch across the continent in a broad belt from Mali and Burkina Faso in the west to Ethiopia and Uganda in the east, and down through Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Malawi in the south.

Many of these countries are among the world's poorest, and their landlocked status is a significant factor. A study published in the Journal of International Economics found that landlocked countries have trade volumes approximately 60 percent lower than comparable coastal nations, even after controlling for other economic factors.

Ethiopia, with a population of over 120 million, is the most populated landlocked country in the world. It lost its coastline when Eritrea gained independence in 1993. Since then, Ethiopia has relied primarily on Djibouti, a small coastal nation, for port access. Over 90 percent of Ethiopia's trade passes through the port of Djibouti, creating a strategic dependency that both countries must carefully manage.

## Europe's Wealthy Exceptions

Not all landlocked countries are poor. In Europe, several landlocked nations are among the wealthiest on the planet. Switzerland, Luxembourg, Liechtenstein, and Austria all lack coastlines but have thriving economies.

The difference is infrastructure and neighbors. European landlocked countries are surrounded by stable, wealthy nations with excellent rail, road, and river networks. Switzerland can ship goods down the Rhine to the North Sea. Austria uses the Danube to connect to the Black Sea. These inland waterways function as highways for heavy cargo, partially compensating for the lack of a coast.

Central Asian landlocked countries like Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan have no such luxury. They are surrounded by other landlocked countries, separated from the nearest ocean by thousands of kilometers of varied terrain and multiple international borders. Uzbekistan and Liechtenstein share the rare distinction of being doubly landlocked: entirely surrounded by other landlocked countries.

## Military and Strategic Implications

Navies require coastlines. Landlocked countries cannot project naval power, which historically limited their military options. But in the modern era, this constraint is less significant than the trade implications.

More pressing is the strategic vulnerability of depending on neighbors for access to the outside world. Bolivia lost its coastal territory to Chile in the War of the Pacific (1879-1884) and has never stopped seeking its return. Bolivia maintains a navy that trains on Lake Titicaca, and its constitution includes a clause asserting the right to sovereign access to the Pacific Ocean. The dispute has been brought before the International Court of Justice multiple times.

Paraguay, landlocked in the heart of South America, fought the devastating Chaco War with Bolivia (1932-1935) partly over access to the Paraguay River, which provides a water route to the Atlantic. Access to navigable waterways is a matter of national survival for landlocked states.

## The Psychology of Being Landlocked

There is a cultural dimension to landlocked geography that is harder to quantify but no less real. Countries with coastlines develop maritime traditions: fishing, sailing, naval exploration, beach tourism. Their national identities often incorporate the sea.

Landlocked nations develop different relationships with their landscapes. Nepal's identity is tied to the Himalayas. Switzerland's is connected to the Alps. Mongolia's vast steppe defines its cultural imagination. These are not lesser identities, but they are fundamentally shaped by the absence of the sea.

Tourism in landlocked countries tends to focus on mountains, lakes, rivers, wildlife, and cultural heritage rather than beach resorts. Some of the world's most spectacular natural landscapes are in landlocked countries: the Himalayas in Nepal, Victoria Falls on the Zambia-Zimbabwe border, the Gobi Desert in Mongolia, and the Swiss Alps.

## A Growing Challenge

Climate change adds another layer of complexity. Many landlocked developing countries are in arid or semi-arid regions that are expected to become drier. Water scarcity, which is already a challenge in Central Asia and the Sahel, will intensify. Without coastline access to desalination plants or maritime resources, landlocked nations must rely entirely on rivers, lakes, and groundwater that are often shared with neighbors.

The economics of being landlocked are unlikely to improve without significant investment in transportation infrastructure, regional trade agreements, and diplomatic stability with transit countries. Some progress is being made. The African Continental Free Trade Area, launched in 2021, aims to reduce trade barriers across the continent, which would disproportionately benefit landlocked African nations.

## Know Your Landlocked Countries

Can you name all 44 landlocked countries? Most people can get the obvious ones: Switzerland, Mongolia, Bolivia. But fewer can name Lesotho (entirely surrounded by South Africa), Eswatini (bordered only by South Africa and Mozambique), or Bhutan (nestled between India and China in the Himalayas).
