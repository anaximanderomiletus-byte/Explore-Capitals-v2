Open a world map and trace the coastlines. Every continent is ringed by nations boasting deep-water ports, sandy beaches, and direct access to the ocean. But look inland, away from the water, and you will find 44 countries completely sealed off from the sea by their neighbors. 

These landlocked nations face a brutal geographic reality. Missing out on a coastline affects everything from the cost of food to military defense and national identity. Here is how living without an ocean shapes a country.

## The Trade Problem

Over 80 percent of global trade travels across the ocean. Massive container ships haul everything from smartphones to grain across the world for a fraction of the cost of airplanes or trucks. Coastal countries simply build a port, dock the ships, and unload their goods directly. Landlocked countries do not have this luxury.

Every single import and export from a landlocked country must physically cross another country's border before it ever reaches a ship. This adds crushing costs, frustrating delays, and constant political risk. If a neighboring country suddenly closes its border, raises transit taxes, or fails to pave its roads, the landlocked nation's entire economy grinds to a halt. 

## Africa's Landlocked Belt

Africa contains 16 landlocked countries, more than any other continent on Earth. They stretch in a broad, dusty belt from Mali in the west to Ethiopia in the east, trailing down through Zambia in the south.

Many of these nations sit among the poorest in the world, and their lack of a coastline is a major reason why. Economists estimate that landlocked countries trade about 60 percent less than similar nations lucky enough to have a coast. 

Take Ethiopia. With over 120 million people, it is the most populated landlocked country on the planet. But it actually used to have a coast! It lost its ocean access when Eritrea fought for and won independence in 1993. Since that day, massive Ethiopia has relied almost entirely on Djibouti—a tiny coastal neighbor—to funnel its goods to the sea.

## Europe's Wealthy Exceptions

Not every landlocked country struggles. In Europe, several landlocked nations rank among the absolute wealthiest on the globe. Switzerland, Luxembourg, Liechtenstein, and Austria all lack coastlines, but their economies hum with power.

Their secret? World-class infrastructure and wealthy, cooperative neighbors. European landlocked countries sit surrounded by stable nations armed with bullet trains and flawless highways. Even better, they use massive, deep rivers like the Rhine and the Danube to float heavy cargo directly out to the ocean. 

Central Asian landlocked countries, like Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, do not enjoy this luxury. Thousands of miles of punishing mountains and harsh deserts separate them from the nearest saltwater port. Uzbekistan and Liechtenstein even share a bizarre geographic title: they are "doubly landlocked," meaning they are completely surrounded by other landlocked countries!

## Military and Strategic Vulnerability

Historically, powerful nations needed a coastline to build navies and project military force. Landlocked countries simply could not build war fleets, severely limiting their global power. 

More importantly, being landlocked makes a country strategically fragile. They remain totally dependent on their neighbors to connect with the outside world. Look at Bolivia. The country lost its precious coastal territory to Chile during a brutal war in the 1880s. Incredibly, Bolivia still maintains a dedicated navy that trains on a high-altitude lake, and the nation continues to loudly demand its right to access the Pacific Ocean.

## The Psychology of Being Landlocked

Lacking a coast changes a country's culture. Nations with beaches naturally develop maritime traditions: deep-sea fishing, sailing, and bustling beach tourism. The ocean weaves itself directly into their national identity.

Landlocked nations must forge entirely different relationships with their geography. Nepal anchors its proud identity to the towering Himalayas. Switzerland defines itself by the snowy Alps. Mongolia's vast, grassy steppes shape its nomadic culture. When tourists visit these countries, they come for the dramatic mountains, mirror-like lakes, and rare wildlife, never missing the beach.

## Key Takeaways
- **No ocean access means higher costs:** Landlocked countries must rely on their neighbors' ports, making it significantly more expensive to import and export goods.
- **Africa holds the most:** With 16 landlocked countries, Africa leads the world in nations cut off from the sea.
- **Rivers offer a lifeline:** Wealthy landlocked nations like Switzerland cleverly use massive river networks to ship goods directly to the ocean.
- **Double landlocked:** Only two countries in the world (Uzbekistan and Liechtenstein) find themselves entirely surrounded by other landlocked nations.
- **A different cultural focus:** Without oceans, landlocked nations build their proud cultural identities around mountains, plains, and lakes instead of seafaring traditions.
