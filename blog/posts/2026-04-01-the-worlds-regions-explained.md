The world is divided into regions, but nobody agrees on what the regions are. The United Nations uses one system. The World Bank uses another. Geographers, political scientists, and aid organizations each draw the lines differently. The result is a set of regional labels that everyone uses but nobody can precisely define.

Understanding these regions, their logic and their contradictions, is one of the most practical skills in geographic literacy.

## Sub-Saharan Africa

Sub-Saharan Africa refers to the 48 countries south of the Sahara Desert. The label distinguishes these nations from North Africa (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt), which is culturally and linguistically more connected to the Arab world. The dividing line runs roughly along the southern edge of the Sahara.

But the boundary is not clean. Mauritania, Mali, Niger, Chad, and Sudan all straddle the Saharan divide, with northern populations that are culturally Arab or Berber and southern populations that are ethnically and linguistically sub-Saharan. Sudan's own north-south division led to the creation of South Sudan in 2011.

The label "sub-Saharan" is itself debated. Critics argue it defines an enormous and diverse region by what it is below rather than what it is. West Africa, East Africa, Southern Africa, and Central Africa are each more geographically and culturally meaningful subdivisions, but the umbrella term persists in international discourse.

## The Middle East

The Middle East is one of the most commonly used and least precisely defined regional labels in geography. At its core, it includes the Arabian Peninsula (Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait), the Levant (Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel, Palestine), Iraq, and Iran. Most definitions also include Egypt and Turkey.

But where does the Middle East end? Is Afghanistan part of it? Pakistan? Libya? The answers vary by institution and context. The US military's Central Command includes Egypt and Central Asia. The World Bank groups North Africa and the Middle East together. Some British usage places the Middle East only east of Egypt and calls everything else "the Near East."

The term itself is Eurocentric, meaning "east" of Europe and "middle" between Europe and the Far East. Alternative terms like "West Asia" are gaining traction in some academic and diplomatic circles, though "Middle East" remains dominant in media and popular usage.

## Southeast Asia

Southeast Asia encompasses 11 countries: Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, the Philippines, Brunei, and Timor-Leste. The region is divided between mainland Southeast Asia (the continental countries) and maritime Southeast Asia (the island and peninsular countries).

The regional identity is formalized through ASEAN (Association of Southeast Asian Nations), which includes 10 of the 11 countries (Timor-Leste has observer status). ASEAN provides a political framework that reinforces the regional label, giving Southeast Asia a coherence that some other regions lack.

The boundary between Southeast Asia and East Asia is generally drawn at China's southern border. The boundary with South Asia is usually placed at Myanmar's western border with Bangladesh and India. But cultural and historical influences blur these lines: Vietnam's Confucian heritage connects it to East Asia, while Myanmar's Buddhist traditions link it to South Asian influence.

## Central America vs. South America

Central America consists of seven countries: Guatemala, Belize, Honduras, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, and Panama. It is geographically part of North America (the continent), but culturally and politically it is often grouped with South America under the label "Latin America."

The boundary between Central and South America is conventionally drawn at the Colombia-Panama border, which follows the Darien Gap, one of the most impassable stretches of terrain in the Americas. There is no road connecting Panama to Colombia, despite the Pan-American Highway running the length of both continents.

The Caribbean is sometimes included in Central America, sometimes treated as a separate region, and sometimes grouped with Latin America. The categorization depends entirely on who is drawing the lines and for what purpose.

## Central Asia

Central Asia typically includes five former Soviet republics: Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyzstan. Some definitions also include Mongolia, Afghanistan, or the western provinces of China. The region is defined by its landlocked geography, its Turkic and Persian linguistic heritage, and its position between Russia, China, and the Middle East.

The Silk Road ran through Central Asia for centuries, making it a crossroads of trade and culture between East and West. Soviet-era borders, drawn with little regard for ethnic or geographic logic, created the complicated boundary patterns that persist today, including enclaves and exclaves that make Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan's border one of the most complex in the world.

## Oceania

Oceania is the most geographically dispersed region, encompassing Australia, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, and roughly 20,000 Pacific islands divided into three subregions: Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia.

The subregions were originally defined by European explorers based on racial classifications and are increasingly questioned for their colonial origins. But the names persist because no widely accepted alternatives have emerged. Melanesia includes Papua New Guinea, Fiji, Solomon Islands, and Vanuatu. Micronesia includes Palau, the Federated States of Micronesia, and the Marshall Islands. Polynesia stretches from New Zealand to Hawaii to Easter Island.

## Why Regions Matter

Regional labels are imperfect, but they are indispensable. They shape how aid is distributed, how news is reported, how textbooks are organized, and how people think about the world. A country's regional classification affects its diplomatic relationships, its eligibility for trade agreements, and even its participation in sporting events.

The labels also carry assumptions. "Developing world," "Global South," "Third World," and "emerging markets" are all regional-adjacent categories that bundle together vastly different countries based on economic criteria that may or may not be meaningful.
