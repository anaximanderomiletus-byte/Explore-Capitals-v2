Russia covers 17.1 million square kilometers, spanning eleven time zones from Kaliningrad on the Baltic to Vladivostok on the Pacific. It is nearly twice the size of the second-largest country, Canada, and almost 50 times the size of Germany. On a map, Russia dominates the northern hemisphere. But most of that space is Siberian taiga, permafrost, and tundra where very few people live.

The relationship between a country's area and its usable land, its population, and its economic output is one of the most instructive disconnects in geography.

## The Top Five

The five largest countries by total area are Russia (17.1 million square kilometers), Canada (10.0 million), the United States (9.8 million), China (9.6 million), and Brazil (8.5 million). Together, they account for roughly 40 percent of the world's total land area.

But their populations tell a different story. China has 1.4 billion people. The United States has 340 million. Brazil has 216 million. Russia has 144 million. Canada has just 40 million. Canada, the second-largest country, has fewer people than California. Its vast northern territories are largely uninhabited tundra and boreal forest.

## Russia: The Empty Giant

Russia's population is concentrated in a relatively narrow band of territory west of the Ural Mountains, centered on Moscow and Saint Petersburg. East of the Urals lies Siberia, which accounts for roughly 77 percent of Russia's land area but holds only about 27 percent of its population.

The emptiness of Siberia is a product of climate. Winter temperatures in central Siberia regularly drop below minus 40 degrees. Permafrost, ground that remains frozen year-round, covers roughly 65 percent of Russian territory. Building infrastructure on permafrost is expensive and unreliable: roads buckle, foundations shift, and pipelines require special insulation. The Trans-Siberian Railway, completed in 1916, remains one of the most ambitious engineering projects ever undertaken to connect this vast space.

## Canada: North of the Treeline

Canada's population hugs the southern border with the United States. Roughly 66 percent of Canadians live within 100 kilometers of the US border. The northern territories, Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut, together cover more than 3.9 million square kilometers but are home to fewer than 120,000 people.

Nunavut alone is larger than Mexico but has a population of roughly 40,000, mostly Inuit. The territory's average temperature is minus 10 degrees Celsius, and much of it is above the Arctic treeline, where no trees grow. Supplying remote communities relies on seasonal sea lifts and expensive air freight.

## Algeria: Africa's Largest

Algeria became Africa's largest country when South Sudan separated from Sudan in 2011. Its 2.4 million square kilometers make it the tenth largest in the world. But roughly 80 percent of Algeria is Saharan desert, and the vast majority of the population lives in the narrow coastal strip along the Mediterranean.

The Saharan interior is home to scattered oasis communities, gas extraction facilities, and some of the most spectacular desert landscapes on Earth, including the Tassili n'Ajjer rock art sites and the Grand Erg Oriental sand sea. But it is fundamentally empty land, too hot and dry for large-scale settlement.

## Australia: The Outback

Australia covers 7.7 million square kilometers, making it the sixth largest country and the only one that is also a continent. Its interior, colloquially known as the Outback, is arid or semi-arid land that supports cattle stations, mining operations, and Indigenous communities but very few people.

Roughly 86 percent of Australians live in urban areas, and the five largest cities (Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, and Adelaide) are all coastal. The concentration is extreme: more people live in the greater Sydney metropolitan area than in the entire state of Western Australia, which covers a third of the continent.

## Small Countries, Big Impact

At the other extreme, some of the world's most influential countries are geographically tiny. The United Kingdom covers 243,000 square kilometers, roughly the size of Michigan. Japan covers 378,000 square kilometers but has the world's third-largest economy. South Korea, at 100,000 square kilometers, is smaller than Iceland but has 50 times its population and one of the most advanced economies on Earth.

Singapore, at just 733 square kilometers, is one of the wealthiest and most globally connected nations. Its entire land area would fit inside the boundaries of most national parks. Size, clearly, is not destiny.

## What Area Tells You

A country's area tells you about its logistical challenges, its climate diversity, its border complexity, and its natural resource base. Large countries tend to have more varied climates, more complex internal transportation networks, and more diverse populations. They also tend to have more empty space, regions that exist on the map but play little role in daily national life.

Understanding the gap between mapped area and lived area is one of the most important geographic skills. The map shows size. Geography shows what that size means.
